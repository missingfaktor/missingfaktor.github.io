Secure the utility sink waste pipework

Posted on 21 August 2026

Two faults were flagged under this sink on an earlier visit and never written down. Something was moving that should have been fixed, and a connection needed something fitted to hold it together. Neither was identified at the time.

1 / 2

The sink drains through a white plastic P-trap with a branch on the left. A grey corrugated appliance hose joins that branch as a rubber cuff pushed over a stepped hose tail. It is reported to run to the washer-dryer in the next cabinet, which is not in shot.

No clip or clamp is visible on that cuff, and the hose doubles back on itself immediately above it, so the bend is working against the joint. That is the strongest candidate for both remembered faults, but a photograph is not a diagnosis and it should not be the only thing checked.

What to establish:

  • Whether that cuff is an ordinary push fit that needs a clip, or a fitting designed to retain the hose without one. Verify it rather than adding a clamp on principle.
  • Whether any part of the white assembly rotates, rocks or slides beyond the normal give of plastic waste. If it does, find the cause — displaced washer, short insertion, cross-threaded nut, poor alignment, hose strain — rather than tightening everything indiscriminately.
  • Whether the appliance hose carries its own weight, or levers on the waste fitting through that bend.
  • Whether the drain hose runs back to the appliance at the height its instructions require, since none of that run is in shot.
  • Whether the white overflow hose on the right, the copper supplies and their isolating valves show any staining or seepage.

The cupboard base is dry and nothing is leaking today. What matters is what washer-dryer vibration and pump pressure do to an unsecured joint over time: a drain hose that works its way off discharges a full wash cycle into the cupboard.

Materials

  • A hose clip sized to the appliance hose tail, if that joint proves to be an ordinary push fit
  • Replacement washers for the trap unions, if any are displaced or perished
  • A clip to support the appliance hose, if its weight is being carried by the waste fitting
DIY fallback Reasonable

Finding out what moves is a hands-on check that risks nothing, and fitting a hose clip is straightforward. The diagnosis is the hard part, not the work. It stops being a DIY job as soon as a joint has to come apart to explain itself.

Fallback approach

  • Clear the cupboard and lay dry paper towel across the base, so any weep shows up later.
  • Take each part of the white assembly in turn and try to move it by hand. Plastic waste always gives a little; note anything that rotates, rocks or slides.
  • Check the grey cuff for a clip hidden on the far side, then feel how firmly it grips the tail. Do not pull it off to find out.
  • Run the washer-dryer to its drain and watch the cuff and the branch while the pump is running.
  • If a plastic nut is loose, nip it up hand-tight only. Grips will crack the nut or the fitting.

Tools and supplies

  • Torch
  • Dry paper towel
  • A nut driver or screwdriver, if a hose clip is fitted

Stop and hand over if

  • Something moves and a loose nut alone does not explain it.
  • A joint has to be dismantled to find out why it moves.
  • The hose tail turns out to need something other than an ordinary hose clip.
  • Water appears at the copper supplies or the isolating valves at the back right.
