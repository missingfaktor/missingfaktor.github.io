Fix pressure loss in the upstairs shower

Posted on 21 August 2026

Running the downstairs shower, or simply opening a tap down there, drops the upstairs shower to almost nothing, and sometimes stops it altogether. On its own the upstairs shower is fine. In practice the two bathrooms cannot be used at the same time.

The upstairs shower room is in the 2023 loft conversion, which makes it the highest outlet in the house. The original property is a bungalow, so nothing about its supply was sized for a second bathroom at roof level. When the main is under load the highest outlet is the first to fail, which is why it is this shower and not the one below.

Hot water comes from a Worcester Bosch Greenstar i combi and there is no cylinder anywhere. So every outlet in the house, hot and cold alike, draws straight off the incoming main, and the hot ones also share the boiler's fixed output. Nothing is stored, so there is no buffer to absorb a second draw.

Three measurements decide which of those is the real limit. Take them in this order:

  1. The incoming main. Static pressure at the stopcock with everything closed, then flow rate into a measured container — first on its own, then with a downstairs tap running. This is the figure that settles whether the supply or the boiler is the constraint, and everything else follows from it.
  2. The boiler's output. The model and its hot water flow rate are on the data plate. A combi of this family delivers somewhere around 10 to 14 litres a minute depending on which one is fitted, against a shower that wants 8 to 10 on its own.
  3. Flow at the upstairs shower, alone and then with a downstairs outlet open. Record whether it runs weak, runs cold, or stops. A combi that falls below its minimum flow stops firing before the water stops, and a thermostatic shower valve closes itself when one supply drops away, so a complete stop is not by itself evidence that the supply reached zero.

What each result points to:

  • A weak incoming main. The supply pipe or a valve on it is the limit. That runs from opening a partly closed stopcock, which costs nothing, to renewing the service pipe in 25 mm, which is the expensive end of the same finding.
  • A healthy main but a boiler-limited hot flow. The combi cannot serve two outlets at once. The remedy is a higher-output boiler, or a move to stored hot water.
  • A healthy main with poor flow only upstairs. This points at the pipework run for the loft conversion — an undersized or over-long 15 mm feed where 22 mm was wanted.

If stored water or an accumulator turns out to be the answer, finding somewhere to put it is part of the job. The loft is now a bedroom and a shower room, so the usual home for a vessel is gone. What remains is the eaves storage strip, at roughly 1 m of head height, and the boxed service stack in the utility room.

DIY fallback Limited

The measuring is DIY, and worth doing before anyone is called out. It costs nothing, it decides which of three very different jobs this turns out to be, and it saves paying someone to establish it. Everything past measuring belongs to a plumber, and the boiler's case should stay shut.

Fallback approach

  • Run the kitchen cold tap full bore into a container of known volume and time it. Litres divided by seconds, multiplied by 60, gives litres a minute. Under about 15 will not comfortably serve two showers.
  • Repeat that measurement with the upstairs shower running, and note how far the kitchen figure falls.
  • Find the internal stopcock and check it is fully open. A valve left part-closed is a common cause and a free fix.
  • Read the model number off the boiler's label without opening the case, and write it down.
  • Run the upstairs shower alone, then have someone open a cold tap downstairs, and record whether it goes weak, goes cold, or stops. That one observation separates a boiler problem from a supply problem.

Tools and supplies

  • A bucket or jug of known volume
  • A stopwatch or phone timer
  • A torch for the stopcock and the boiler label
  • A second person to open a tap downstairs

Stop and hand over if

  • The stopcock is stiff, weeping, or will not turn.
  • The work would mean opening the boiler, altering pipework, or fitting a pump or a vessel.
  • The measurements point at the incoming service pipe, which brings in the water company as well as a plumber.
