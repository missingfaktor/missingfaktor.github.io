Sand and seal wooden countertop and island

Posted on 22 July 2026

The timber worktop and island surfaces are rough and mark easily, and water around the sink has left dark staining.

1 / 2

The area to be treated:

  • Island: 1.9 × 1.2 m, or 2.28 m².
  • Counter run: 2.2 × 0.6 m, or 1.32 m².
  • 3.60 m² gross, and roughly 3.0 to 3.2 m² of actual timber once the hob and sink cut-outs are subtracted.

Whether the surfaces are solid timber or veneer has not been established, and nothing should be machine sanded until it is. An exposed cross-section settles it: the underside of the sink or hob cut-out, the underside of the island overhang, or any chipped or unfinished edge. The visible front edge on its own proves little, because a board core can carry a glued strip of real timber. Narrow staves or finger joints are not evidence of veneer.

On confirmed solid timber, sand evenly with the grain and stop at the grit the species calls for rather than the finest to hand. Around P120 suits an absorbent hardwood such as oak or beech, and P150 is the practical limit on a softwood: a finer surface absorbs less oil. Any existing varnish or lacquer has to come off completely, and the whole sequence should be tested in a hidden area first.

Finish with two or three thin coats of clear matt, food-safe hardwax worktop oil such as Osmo TopOil 3058. At roughly 25 to 30 m² per litre per coat, a 500 ml tin covers this area comfortably. The oil needs eight to ten hours between coats, so the work spans more than one day, and the surface should be kept dry and lightly used for the two to three weeks it takes to cure.

Materials

  • Sander with dust extraction
  • P120 and P150 sanding discs, with P80 only if the test area calls for it
  • Osmo TopOil 3058 Clear Matt or equivalent, 500 ml
  • Applicator pads, brush and lint-free cloths
  • Masking materials
DIY fallback Reasonable

This is a realistic DIY finishing job if both surfaces are confirmed as solid timber. Veneer or an unidentified existing coating changes the risk and should be resolved before machine sanding. Allow more than one day, since the oil needs eight to ten hours between coats.

Fallback approach

  • Confirm the construction at an exposed cross-section — the underside of the sink or hob cut-out, the underside of the island overhang, or a chipped edge — and test the full sanding-and-oiling sequence in a hidden area.
  • Remove or mask adjacent fittings, extract dust at source and sand evenly with the grain, keeping the sander flat and moving rather than tilting onto an edge or working at a single mark.
  • Finish at around P120 on oak or beech and no finer than P150 on a softwood, dropping to P80 only where a confirmed solid surface still holds an old coating.
  • Hand-sand corners and the areas around the sink and hob that the machine cannot reach safely.
  • Remove all dust, then apply thin coats of clear matt food-safe worktop oil with lint-free pads, leaving eight to ten hours between coats.
  • Wash oil-soaked cloths straight away or seal them in an airtight container. Left in a pile or a bin they can ignite on their own.
  • Keep the surface dry and lightly used for the two to three weeks the finish takes to cure.

Tools and supplies

  • 125 mm random-orbit sander with dust extraction
  • P120 and P150 discs, with P80 held in reserve for the test area
  • Hand-sanding blocks and pads
  • Wet-and-dry vacuum
  • Masking materials and lint-free applicators
  • Nitrile gloves, eye protection, hearing protection and an FFP3 mask

Stop and hand over if

  • The surface is veneer, becomes patchy or shows a thin top layer.
  • Deep staining, softness or blackening suggests water damage rather than a surface-marking problem.
  • The existing finish reacts badly, clogs the abrasive heavily or cannot be identified.
